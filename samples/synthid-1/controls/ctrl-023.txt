The wristwatch has come to be an essential component in our lives today, yet it is only a relatively recent innovation on the watchmaking landscape as compared with its land-lubbing brethren. From being merely a novelty item intended solely for men until World War I and then transitioning into something more universally wearable post-war – this fascinating piece of machinery has undergone dramatic changes over time.

One need look no further than the origins of mechanical watches themselves to understand their significance in developing the concept we now call the wristlet or wristwatch (as they were referred to before). The earliest watches were made specifically for horseback riders, mounted knights, hunters and military leaders who needed reliable chronometers while out riding through forests. By necessity, these early portable devices had to contain small components like pendulums, gears, weights, springs, and escapements; but most importantly, each part was handcrafted by skilled artisans rather than mass-produced on assembly lines due to manufacturing limitations at the time.

This first iteration of portable clocks eventually gave way to smaller pocket watches worn vertically around one's neck or hanging from a sash, becoming standard issue equipment among gentlemen of leisure during the Renaissance period onwards. It was not uncommon for them to appear prominently in formal portraits alongside other finery such as powdered wigs, ruffles, lace collars and elaborate jewelry pieces which showcased status within society - think of those depicted in Botticelli’s Venus and Mercury frescoes. 

The era following the Industrial Revolution marked another crucial turning point in how people interacted with their precious metaltimepieces since the advent of interchangeable parts meant cheaper production could lead to increased availability outside elite circles too. Watches became increasingly affordable and practical enough to start appearing in women's fashion collections, albeit initially limited mostly to aristocratic ladies thanks to societal stigma against wearing things deemed "too masculine" publicly. However, even when restricted largely just to high society beauties, pocket watches still remained awkwardly inconvenient items best suited indoors where horses couldn't carry them safely under saddles. As a result, the design problem facing watchmakers remained unsolved until advancements finally caught up technologically speaking. 

It wasn't until after World War II that real progress began towards making portable precision mechanisms compact enough for comfortable wear all day without having to be carried in pockets anymore. With soldiers returning home armed and trained in cutting-edge gadgetry brought back from overseas theaters, consumer demand started increasing sharply once again throughout the late 1940s and 5os. Companies invested heavily in researching new materials capable of sustaining shockwaves without compromising accuracy, producing lightweight alloys resistant to corrosion and magnetic fields while ensuring powertrain efficiency regardless of external conditions experienced daily upon streetscapes across cities worldwide.

By mid-century, major brands including Rolex, Omega, Patek Philippe et al had developed robust automatic winding systems powered purely by body motion so users wouldn't have to constantly fiddle endlessly rewinding mainsprings. These features allowed designers free reign creatively regarding form factors thereafter without fear disrupting delicate internal workings inside slim cases sporting polished finishes reflective off shiny metals or glassy surfaces depending upon aesthetics desired according to prevailing trends dictated annually amidst haute couture houses catering mainly toward wealthy clientele whose wealth often stemmed directly from wartime profits realized selling arms abroad prior
