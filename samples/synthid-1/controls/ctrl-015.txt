Throughout the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, zoo architecture has undergone significant changes as our understanding of animals’ needs evolved alongside public perceptions of animal welfare. This evolution is deeply rooted in societal values regarding conservation, care, education, entertainment, and human-animal interaction.

Initially, early European enclosures were small shelters designed to accommodate and display exotic creatures from far-off lands for scientific exploration and recreation. Early nineteenth-century zoological gardens like London’s Regent's Park Zoo focused on breeding rather than exhibiting large mammals and birds for their rarity or peculiarities; however, these facilities often neglected proper conditions such as space, temperature control, food supply, clean water access, enrichment materials, and veterinary health checks due to limited knowledge and financial constraints.

Over time, there was an increasing focus on maintaining adequate living spaces for captive wildlife. The introduction of artificial lighting provided a solution to natural light variations outside during winter months while allowing controlled temperatures year-round—crucial elements for some species sensitive to climate change. Improved nutrition included processed foods tailored to specific dietary requirements, enhancing overall animal wellbeing through balanced diets rich in proteins, carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals, fiber, moisture retention aids (such as hay), supplements against malnutrition risks posed by lack of variety inherent with imported ingredients.

Another area transformed significantly involves providing mental stimulation via environmental enrichment initiatives meant to prevent boredom among the housed animals. Incorporating complex structures for climbing, hiding, digging, problem-solving games, scent markers, and socializing companions ensured higher cognitive engagement, reducing stress levels compared to simple concrete pools or barren cages filled merely with exhibits showcasing physical traits of different fauna types without context-specific features.

Public demand also played a vital role in shaping modern zoo practices towards more humane standards.
